Most new-grad offers are contingent on a pre-employment screening that includes identity, education, and employment verification, sometimes a criminal background check, and employment-eligibility verification. In the United States, Form I-9 generally applies to all new employees, including citizens. The exact scope and timing vary by employer, screening vendor, role, and jurisdiction, so treat the specifics here as orientation rather than a universal timeline.

## What most pre-employment screenings cover

The components vary. A typical new-grad screening includes some combination of:

- **Identity verification.** Identity information requested through the employer’s verified screening process. For U.S. payroll, an ITIN does not replace an SSN. If your SSN is pending, tell HR; see [IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/hiring-employees).
- **Education verification.** The school confirms degree, major, and graduation date.
- **Employment verification.** Past internships and jobs confirm title and dates.
- **Criminal background.** A county or federal check; specifics vary by state, by role, and by employer policy.
- **Credit check.** Uncommon for new-grad engineering roles; more common in finance and certain senior roles.
- **Reference checks.** Some employers treat this as part of the screening rather than the interview loop.

The screening is usually run by a third-party vendor. The exact deliverable back to the employer (a pass/fail/consider verdict, a risk tier, an adjudicated report) depends on the vendor and the employer's policy. In the United States, an employer using a background-reporting company must obtain written permission. Before an adverse decision based on the report, it must provide the report and a summary of FCRA rights, allowing you to review it and raise errors; additional notice follows an adverse decision. Local rules may add protections. See [FTC guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/employer-background-checks-and-your-rights). These U.S. rules do not describe Canadian or Indian screening law.

## Timing

There is no single "typical" timeline. Some screenings complete in a few business days; others take several weeks. Specific timing depends on:

- The employer's policy on when to initiate the screening (some initiate at offer, others after acceptance; some wait for a specific document).
- The vendor's process and queue.
- The responsiveness of the institutions being checked (registrars, prior employers).
- Whether anything needs to be re-verified because of name or address mismatches.

If you have an offer with a known deadline (relocation, visa cap, lease start), tell the recruiter early. Ask which steps can run in parallel and who can confirm the expected completion date.

## What to do during the screening

- **Reply to every email from the screening vendor quickly.** Confirm requests through the employer or vendor’s verified portal before sending sensitive documents.
- **Tell your university career center or registrar.** Many schools have a process for "employer verification" requests; using it shortens the wait.
- **Have your prior manager's contact ready.** A previous employer who does not respond to verification emails is one of the most common delays.
- **Keep your information current.** Tell HR or the vendor about changed names, addresses or identifiers and ask which records need updating.

If your employment authorization is relevant to the role, the employer will have its own process for completing required work-authorization verification. Follow the employer's instructions and use official USCIS and DHS guidance for questions about your status. See [OPT and STEM OPT](../opt-and-stem-opt/) and the [official sources section](../opt-and-stem-opt/) at the bottom of that guide.

## Work-authorization checks

U.S. employers generally complete Form I-9 for every new employee. Employees choose acceptable documents from the official lists; the employer cannot demand a particular document solely because of nationality or immigration status. E-Verify is used by participating employers, including STEM OPT employers. It is an employment-verification process, not a background check or a visa application. See [DOJ employee-rights guidance](https://www.justice.gov/crt/immigrant-and-employee-rights-section). See [OPT and STEM OPT](../opt-and-stem-opt/) for the broader context and the official USCIS and Study in the States sources.

What candidates can do:

- Review the [Form I-9 acceptable documents](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/form-i-9-acceptable-documents) and choose a valid document combination for your situation. Immigration application documents and I-9 evidence are not interchangeable.
- Respond quickly to questions about dates and document numbers.
- For STEM OPT, expect a Form I-983 training plan to be part of the onboarding; your DSO and the employer both have roles here.

## If something flags

A flag is not a fail. Many flags are address mismatches, common-name false positives, or minor discrepancies that the vendor will ask you to confirm. Reply with the corrected information; the screening continues.

Answer lawful questions accurately and correct material errors. Do not assume that every record must be volunteered: local rules can limit questions about criminal history, sealed records or expungements. If a report is inaccurate, dispute it with the reporting company and inform the employer. Get jurisdiction-specific legal advice if unsure what disclosure is required.

## What this means for your start date

Build a buffer between accepting and your planned start, especially if your start date is the first of the month or tied to a lease, visa, or relocation window. Ask HR whether the start date can move and get any change in writing. ## Where to get authoritative answers

For immigration and work-authorization questions:

- **[USCIS](https://www.uscis.gov/).** Forms, policy manual, processing times.
- **[DHS Study in the States](https://studyinthestates.dhs.gov/).** F-1, OPT, STEM OPT, Form I-983.
- **[E-Verify](https://www.e-verify.gov/).** Employer-side program information; employee rights and myE-Verify.
- **Your university's Designated School Official (DSO).** Case-specific interpretation for your program.
- **A qualified immigration attorney.** For case-specific legal questions.

The board flags stated sponsorship and citizenship restrictions in postings. See [Visa sponsorship and citizenship flags](../visa-sponsorship-and-citizenship/) for what the flags mean, and [Sponsorship language](../sponsorship-language/) for the phrases that show up in postings.
