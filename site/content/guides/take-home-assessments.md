Take-home and work-sample assessments ask you to build something small at home over hours or days. The brief usually states the time budget, the deliverables, and the rules around tools (including AI). Read it twice before you start. For the rules around AI in particular, see [AI coding assessments](../ai-coding-assessments/) and [Interview tool policies](../interview-tool-policies/).

## What employers are actually scoring

Useful questions to check against the employer's rubric:

1. **Does the submission match the brief?** Did you deliver what was asked, in the format requested, within the time budget?
2. **Is the code or work defensible?** Can you walk the reviewer through it and explain the choices? The follow-up interview is part of the test.
3. **Would this person ship something similar in production?** Does it look like the work of someone who could do this job?

The scoring order, partial credit, and follow-up format vary. Ask which requirements are essential and what trade-offs the reviewer expects.

## How to read the brief

Look for:

- **Time budget.** Ask for the expected time commitment. If none is given, agree on a time box and document any unfinished scope.
- **Required deliverables.** A repo, a recording, a write-up, a deployed URL. Each is checked. Missing one is the easiest way to be screened out.
- **Allowed tools and resources.** AI allowed or not. Specific libraries forbidden. Datasets you must use.
- **Submission format.** A specific URL, a zip file, an email. Use the format requested.
- **Deadline.** Some take-homes have a hard window; some are open. If open, do not over-polish — submit something defensible.

If the brief is ambiguous on any of these, ask the recruiter. Five minutes saved at the start is thirty minutes saved at the end.

## How to deliver well

- **Spend a proportionate part of the stated time budget on a plan.** A short README in the repo that names the problem, the approach, the trade-offs you considered, and what you would change with more time. Reviewers read this first.
- **Match the deliverable to the format.** If the brief says "deploy a URL", deploy a URL. If it says "record a five-minute walkthrough", record one. Don't surprise the reviewer with a different format even if you think yours is better.
- **Write the smallest end-to-end thing that works.** Then add one or two things you decided to add. The reviewer would rather see something finished and one thing well than five things half-finished.
- **Leave a clean trail.** Git history that shows your reasoning, comments where the design choice was non-obvious, a short list of things you would do next.

## How to defend in the follow-up

If there is a follow-up walkthrough, prepare for questions such as:

- Why did you pick this approach?
- What did you cut, and why?
- What would you change with another week?
- Where would this break in production?

Prepare those four answers in advance. They are the take-home follow-up, repeated. See [How to handle an interview question you don't know](../how-to-handle-an-interview-question-you-dont-know/) for the broader interview-handling moves when a follow-up surprises you.

## What to do with a long take-home

If the brief is for more than ten hours, decide before you start whether you will do it at all. Consider the opportunity cost, the expected evaluation process, and whether the work resembles unpaid production work. A take-home you finish is better than a take-home you start. If you choose not to do it, that is information; the company has told you how they value your time, and you have decided whether to play.
