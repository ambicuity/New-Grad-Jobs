Reaching the onsite but not the offer is one of the most common new-grad outcomes, and one of the easiest to diagnose. The data is in the rejection itself: the round you lost on, what the recruiter says, and what changed between loops.

## Map where you are losing

If you have done three or more onsites and gotten no offer, write down for each one which round ended it. Patterns:**
- Always the recruiter screen → your story or your logistics are off.
- Always the technical round → your interview coding is below bar.
- Always the behavioral or values round → your STAR stories are too rehearsed or too short.
- Different rounds each time → the loop is fine, but the debriefs do not aggregate to a hire.

Each pattern has a different fix.

## Always the recruiter screen

The recruiter screen is fifteen minutes of motivation, logistics, and a two-minute summary of who you are. Three common misses:

- **The summary is too vague.** "I'm a CS student who likes to code" is not a summary. Two minutes of the project you are most proud of, what you want next, and why this role. Rehearse it; see [Behavioral interviews](../behavioral-interviews/).
- **You are not aligned with the role.** If the recruiter asks "why this role?" and you say "I just want any new-grad role", the answer ends the screen. Read the team's page; mention one specific thing.
- **Logistics block you.** Salary, location, start date. The recruiter is checking whether they can hire you. Decide these before the call.

## Always the technical round

If you reach the technical round and fail it twice, the problem is rarely "you cannot code." It is usually one of:

- **You go silent.** Interviewers score on what you say while you think. See [How to handle an interview question you don't know](../how-to-handle-an-interview-question-you-dont-know/) and [How to prepare for an interview in 7 days](../how-to-prepare-for-an-interview-in-7-days/).
- **You solve a different problem.** The interviewer often steers you; if you do not adjust, you miss the round.
- **You do not test your code.** Two off-by-one errors that a thirty-second trace would catch lose the round. Always trace one or two examples before you say you are done.

Practice is the fix. Time-box yourself on a LeetCode problem for thirty-five minutes, narrate out loud, and trace one example. See [LeetCode roadmap for new grads](../leetcode-roadmap-for-new-grads/).

## Always the behavioral or values round

The most common miss: the stories are too short. A two-minute answer that hits STAR and ends with a reflection is harder than it sounds; see [Behavioral interviews](../behavioral-interviews/) for the structure and the reflection step most candidates skip.

The second common miss: the stories are too polished. Interviewers at new grad level are scoring self-awareness more than perfection. A story where the result was bad and the reflection is real is one of the strongest answers available.

## Different rounds each time

This is the hardest pattern. You are clearing individual bars but not aggregating to a hire. Two things to try:

- **Ask the recruiter.** After an onsite, ask "what feedback can you share?" Some will; some will not. Any feedback is data.
- **Pick a smaller role to convert.** If you cannot convert at the largest companies on your list, convert at a smaller one. Read [Startup vs enterprise](../startup-vs-enterprise/) for the trade-off, and [How to evaluate an offer](../evaluating-an-offer/) for what to look for.

## When it is the loop as a whole

If three loops in a row end with "we went with another candidate", the issue may be your interview presence, not any single round. Ask a friend to do a full mock loop with you, then sit with you for the feedback. The fix is usually one of: pace (too fast or too slow), confidence (you apologise more than you explain), or specificity (your answers are general when they should be about you).

If the data says you have done five loops with no offers, change something material. The loop is a numbers game at your level; five is enough signal.
