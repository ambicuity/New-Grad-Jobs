Cover letters and short-answer questions take time, so spend it where an application asks for them. This guide is for graduates in any field. It covers when to write one, a short structure, and how to build "why this company" and "why this role" answers from the posting and the employer's own pages. It ends with templates you can fill in. Nothing here can predict how a particular employer weighs a letter, so follow each application's instructions.

## When a cover letter matters

- **The posting requires one.** Write it. Some applications list it as a required document. Penn State Health's administrative fellowship, for example, asks for a cover letter, a personal statement, recommendation letters and transcripts, and says [applications missing required documents won't be considered](https://med.psu.edu/residencies-fellowships/professional-programs/administrative-fellowship).
- **The form has an optional field.** Decide based on whether you have something specific to add that your résumé does not show, such as a relocation plan, a career change, a gap, or a direct link between a project and the team's work.
- **There is no field.** Do not force a letter in. Put your effort into the résumé and any questions on the form.

Your time is limited, so write full letters for the roles you most want. [Application strategy](../application-strategy/) explains how to split your effort between priority roles and broader applications.

## A short structure

University career offices describe much the same shape. MIT's career office recommends [no more than one page](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/how-to-write-an-effective-cover-letter/), with an opening, two or three body paragraphs giving examples from your experience, and a closing. It also says each letter should be tailored to the company you are writing to.

1. **Opening (two or three sentences).** The role, including any requisition ID, and one specific reason you are applying.
2. **Evidence (one or two paragraphs).** Two requirements from the posting, each matched to something you have done. Say what you did and what happened. Add detail your résumé does not have, rather than repeating it.
3. **Fit (two or three sentences).** One thing about the employer or team, taken from their own pages, and why it connects to your work.
4. **Close (one or two sentences).** Thanks, and your availability or authorization if the posting asks for it.

Address the hiring manager by name if the posting gives one. Otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager" is fine. Do not search for private contact details.

## Build "why this company" and "why this role" from evidence

Generic praise ("an industry leader with a great culture") tells the reader nothing. Build the answer from three sources you can cite:

- **The posting.** Which duties or tools match work you have done? Quote the posting's own terms where they are accurate for you.
- **The employer's own pages.** Product pages, program pages, annual reports, engineering or research blogs, published values or patient-care commitments. Pick one concrete item.
- **Your own experience.** One project, job, course or volunteer role that connects the first two.

Then write one sentence for each: "The posting asks for X. I did Y. That connects to Z, which the employer describes on its site." If you cannot find a concrete Z, say less rather than inventing interest. [Tailoring your résumé](../tailoring-your-resume/) uses the same posting analysis for the résumé.

## Short-answer application questions

Many forms ask a few written questions instead of a letter. Answer the question asked, keep within any word limit, and use one example per answer.

| Question type | What a good answer contains |
| --- | --- |
| Why this role / why us | The posting, employer and experience sentences from the section above |
| Describe a challenge or a project | Situation, your own action, the result, what you would repeat |
| Teamwork or conflict | Your part, how you disagreed or coordinated, what happened |
| Eligibility, location, start date | A plain, accurate answer. Do not guess about work authorization |
| Anything else to share | Optional. Use it for a fact that changes how the application reads, or leave it blank |

For the challenge and teamwork questions, a ready set of stories saves time. [Behavioral story bank builder](../behavioral-story-bank-builder/) helps you prepare them. For sponsorship and authorization questions, see [Sponsorship language](../sponsorship-language/).

## Templates

Replace every bracket. Delete any line that does not apply to you.

### Cover letter

> Dear [Hiring Manager or name],
>
> I am applying for the [role title] position ([requisition ID, if listed]). [One specific reason: a product, program, team or problem from the posting or employer's site] is the kind of work I want to do, and my [degree, project or job] has prepared me for it.
>
> The posting asks for [requirement 1]. In [project, course or job], I [what you did], which [result, stated plainly]. It also asks for [requirement 2]. As [role or setting], I [what you did], and [result].
>
> I am drawn to [employer] because [one concrete item from their own pages], and [how it connects to your experience or goal].
>
> Thank you for considering my application. I am available to start [date or term], and [authorization or location statement, only if the posting asks].
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your name]

### Why this company (about 100 words)

> The posting describes [duty or problem]. I worked on [similar task] in [project or job], where I [action and result]. [Employer]'s [specific product, program or published commitment] connects to that work because [reason]. I want a first role where I can [specific skill to build], and this team's [detail from the posting] would let me do that.

### Describe a project (about 150 words)

> In [setting and date], I [built, analysed, organised or wrote] [thing] to [goal]. My part was [your specific contribution]. The hardest step was [obstacle], which I handled by [action]. The result was [outcome, stated accurately]. If I did it again, I would [one improvement].

## Using AI on letters and answers

AI can help you brainstorm, check grammar or tighten a draft you wrote. It cannot know your experience. MIT's career office describes AI as [a brainstorming partner, not a ghostwriter](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/using-ai-for-writing-cover-letters/), and says you are responsible for every claim being accurate.

::: evidence practical
Before you submit, read each sentence and ask whether you could explain it in an interview. Delete any sentence you could not explain, or that names a skill, tool or result you do not have.
:::

Do not paste private information into tools you have not checked, and follow any rule the employer states about AI. [AI-assisted applications: what not to automate](../ai-applications-what-not-to-automate/) sets out the line between drafting help and misrepresentation.

## Before you submit

- The company name, role title and requisition ID are correct everywhere.
- Every claim matches your résumé and is true.
- The letter is one page or less, and each answer is within its limit.
- The file name and format match the instructions, for example `Lastname_Firstname_CoverLetter.pdf`.
- You saved a copy alongside the posting, so you can reread it before an interview.
