If you are entering a field from another career, a different major or a bootcamp, you are new to the field but not new to work. Your past is a transferable record, not a liability. The search has two jobs: confirm you are eligible for the roles you want, and show evidence of the new field's core work alongside what you bring from the old one. This applies whether you are moving into software, nursing, accounting, sales, logistics or anything else.

## Check eligibility first

Some requirements decide whether you can be hired at all. Read each posting for them before you tailor anything; [Are you qualified?](../are-you-qualified/) separates firm requirements from soft ones.

- **Licenses and registrations.** Nursing, teaching, public accounting (CPA), some engineering and many healthcare roles require a license or a route to one. The rules are set by the licensing body where you will work, not by the employer.
- **Specific degrees.** Regulated roles may require a particular degree. "Bachelor's degree or equivalent experience" is different from "Bachelor's degree in accounting".
- **Graduation windows.** Some new-grad programs accept only people who graduated in a stated period. If you graduated years ago, look for "entry level" or "associate" postings that ask about experience, not graduation date.
- **Work authorization and citizenship.** These apply to everyone; the board flags stated restrictions.

::: evidence official
Licensing is decided by the regulator, not the job board. For US nursing, NCSBN says [NCLEX registration](https://www.ncsbn.org/exams.page) includes the nursing regulatory body, and candidates must meet that regulator's requirements in the place they plan to practice. Check the licensing body in your target field and jurisdiction.
:::

Degree requirements are also not fixed across employers. Some public employers have stated policies on them; for example, Pennsylvania's governor [announced in January 2023](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/oa/newsroom/governor-shapiro-signs-first-executive-order-announcing-92-percent-of-commonwealth-jobs-do-not-require-a-four-year-degree) that most Commonwealth jobs do not require a four-year degree and directed agencies to emphasize skills and experience. That is one employer's policy. Read each posting.

## Know how much preparation the field expects

Before committing to a target, check how much education and training the occupation usually asks of new hires. O*NET groups occupations into [Job Zones](https://www.onetonline.org/help/online/zones); for example, it says most Job Zone Four occupations require a four-year bachelor's degree, but some do not, and most Job Zone Five occupations require graduate school. That tells you whether a short course is likely to be enough, or whether the realistic route is a degree or license first.

## Inventory your transferable evidence

Write one row for each piece of prior work or study. Then add a second column for what the new field values.

| Prior experience | What you actually did | What the new field values | Evidence you can show |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restaurant manager | Scheduled 15 staff, ordered stock, handled complaints | Operations, logistics: planning, vendors, service | Schedules, stock sheets, a manager reference |
| Lab technician | Ran assays, kept records to protocol | Quality, data analysis: accuracy, documentation | SOPs you followed, a supervisor reference |
| Teacher | Planned lessons, tracked student data | Training, customer success, instructional design | Lesson plans, anonymized progress tracking |
| Bootcamp graduate | Built three web apps in a team | Software: finished, explainable projects | Repositories, deployed demos |

The rows are illustrations. Fill yours with what you did, at the scale you did it, and run each claim through [the résumé evidence test](../resume-evidence-test/). O*NET's occupation pages, such as the [retail salespersons summary](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/41-2031.00), list tasks and transferable skills you can use as vocabulary for your old role.

## Add evidence of the new field

Transferable skills explain what you bring; new-field evidence shows you can do the new work. Close the gap with one or two finished pieces of work in the target domain:

- **A project.** [New grad projects that count](../new-grad-projects/) covers what helps, and for software, [what makes a GitHub repo impressive](../what-makes-a-github-repo-impressive/).
- **A credential the field recognizes.** Only where postings ask for it. Check postings first, then choose.
- **Bridging work in your current job.** An analyst task, an automation, a training session. It is real experience you can describe.

For bootcamp graduates, the bootcamp is the training; the projects are the evidence. List the program under education, and put the projects in their own section with links.

## Target employers that fit a switch

Different employers tolerate different gaps. A good target list for a switcher favors:

- **Roles where your old field is an advantage.** A former nurse moving into health-tech support, a former accountant into finance systems, a former teacher into training. Your domain knowledge is the edge.
- **Employers whose postings accept "or equivalent experience"**, or that name skills rather than a degree.
- **The right employer type.** [Startup vs enterprise](../startup-vs-enterprise/) explains how different employers weigh a non-standard background.
- **Adjacent roles** that use your background while you build new-field evidence; see [Adjacent technical roles](../adjacent-technical-roles/).

[Build a target-company list](../build-a-target-company-list/) explains how to tier and refresh the list.

## Tell the story briefly

A recruiter will ask why you are switching. Two sentences, true, and pointed at the role:

> I spent four years running logistics for a restaurant group, and the part I liked most was the scheduling and supply planning. I've since completed a supply chain course and built a demand forecast for my current employer, and that's the work this role does.

Do not hide the earlier career or blur dates; employers may verify employment history. Your earlier work is often what makes you a stronger candidate than someone with the same new-field skills and no other job.

## A checklist before you apply

1. Confirm licenses, degree requirements, graduation windows and authorization for each target posting.
2. Check the occupation's typical preparation on O*NET.
3. Fill in the transferable-evidence worksheet.
4. Finish one piece of new-field evidence.
5. Build a target list weighted toward roles that value your background.
6. Rehearse your two-sentence story.
