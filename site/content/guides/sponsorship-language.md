Postings talk about work authorization in a small number of stock phrases, and the phrases are not all equivalent. Knowing them saves you from applying to roles you cannot take and from skipping roles you could.

## Phrases that mean no sponsorship

- "We are unable to sponsor" / "cannot sponsor" / "will not sponsor" a work visa, now or in the future
- "No sponsorship available for this position"
- "Must be authorized to work in the US without sponsorship"

These are a policy for the role. The board sets its **no sponsorship** flag when it reads phrases like these, and the "no restriction stated" chip hides the posting.

## Phrases that sound stricter than they are

- **"Must be authorized to work in the United States."** On its own, without "without sponsorship", this is true of every legal hire and is often boilerplate. A student on OPT is authorized. The board treats an explicit "authorization required" as a restriction because in practice it usually accompanies a no-sponsorship policy, so check the full sentence.
- **"Legally eligible to work in Canada"** or the Indian equivalents follow the same pattern.
- **"Sponsorship considered on a case-by-case basis."** Not a no. Ask.
- **"Must be able to obtain a work permit."** Depends on the country and on you.

## Phrases that mean citizenship

- "US citizenship required" / "must be a US citizen" / "US persons only"
- "Ability to obtain and maintain a security clearance", "active clearance required", "Secret", "Top Secret", "TS/SCI"
- References to export control or ITAR often come with a US-person requirement

The board sets its **citizenship required** flag on these. Almost all of them come from defense, aerospace, government contracting and some hardware employers; [Citizenship and clearance](../citizenship-and-clearance/) explains why.

## Silence

Most postings say nothing at all. The board shows them as "no restriction stated", which is a description of the text, not a promise from the employer. Silence at a large technology company usually means a policy exists and is applied at offer stage; silence at a small company often means nobody has thought about it. Either way the question belongs in the first recruiter conversation. [OPT and STEM OPT](../opt-and-stem-opt/) lists the questions.

## How the flags are set

The scraper looks for these phrases in the title and description as whole phrases, not substrings, so "sponsorship" inside "our sponsorship program for conferences" does not trip it, but a posting that phrases its policy unusually may go unflagged. When a flag is present it is reliable; when it is absent, read the posting. The exact phrase lists are in the scraper's source on [GitHub](https://github.com/ambicuity/New-Grad-Jobs).

## What to do with a flagged posting

If it says no sponsorship and you will need it eventually, and the role is on OPT-eligible terms, you can still apply if you are willing to take a job that may end when OPT ends. Some graduates do this deliberately for the experience. Go in with your eyes open and a plan for the following year.
