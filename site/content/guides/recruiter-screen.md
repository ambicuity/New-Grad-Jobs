The recruiter screen is the conversation most candidates underestimate. It is not a "chat". It is a fit conversation with specific signals: can the company move on you, can you move on the company, are there flags that make either answer no. Most recruiter screens have a short, predictable list of questions. Preparation is rehearsed answers, not improvisation.

::: evidence practical
A screen often covers fit and logistics, but can include technical or domain questions. Confirm the format and prepare relevant evidence rather than assuming every screen has the same scope.
:::

## The setup

A recruiter screen is a phone or video call, with a duration set by the employer, with a recruiter or a coordinator (not the hiring manager). The recruiter has a checklist: eligibility, location, salary expectations, start date, role fit, team fit. They are usually checking whether to pass you forward, not whether to hire you.

Some screens are with the hiring manager directly. The format is similar but the audience is different — the hiring manager is also evaluating your judgement and your communication, not just your fit.

## The questions

Possible questions include the following. The exact wording varies; the underlying signal does not.

- Tell me about yourself.
- Walk me through your résumé.
- Why this company?
- Why this role?
- What are your salary expectations?
- Where are you based? Are you open to relocation?
- What is your work authorisation? Will you now or in the future require sponsorship?
- What is your earliest start date?
- What other interviews or offers do you have?
- Why are you looking?
- Do you have any questions for me?

The recruiter may also ask about gaps in your résumé, your education and your visa status. See [International student job search center](../international-student-job-search-center/) for the sponsorship-specific guidance.

## The 30 / 60 / 120-second answers

For each question above, prepare three versions. The short version for an unexpected moment, the medium version for the actual screen, the long version for an interview that has space. The three versions share the same content; only the depth changes.

### Tell me about yourself

**30 seconds.** "[Name], graduating [month year] from [school] with [degree]. [One-line role interest]. [One-line relevant project or experience]. Looking for [role family] where I can [one outcome you'd contribute to]."

**60 seconds.** Same opening, then add a sentence on what you did that proves the role interest, and a sentence on why this company specifically.

**120 seconds.** Same opening, then walk through the three experiences that map most clearly to the role: a project, an internship, a course or extracurricular. End with the same closer.

### Why this company?

**30 seconds.** "[Company]'s [specific product, paper, decision or team] is what I'm trying to learn from. [One sentence on why it is the right fit for your stage]."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then add one more specific thing — a recent product launch, a tech blog post, an open-source contribution — and connect it to what you would work on.

**120 seconds.** Same opener, then add a third concrete reason. Specific beats generic. "Great culture" is not a reason; "your eng blog post on [X] is the closest thing I've found to how I want to think about [Y]" is.

### Why this role?

**30 seconds.** "I'm looking for a [role family] role where I can [specific outcome]. This role is [specific fit]. I'm not looking for [specific anti-fit]."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then add a sentence on what you would work on in the first six months, drawn from the posting.

**120 seconds.** Same opener, then connect the role to one or two skills you have already demonstrated.

### Salary expectations

**30 seconds.** "Based on my research and the role, I'm targeting [range]. I'm flexible depending on the overall package and the role."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then add one sentence on what you have learned from [How to research your market salary](../compensation-research/).

**120 seconds.** Same opener, then add a sentence on what the package would need to include for you to consider a lower base (e.g. equity, signing bonus, relocation).

If the recruiter gives you a number first, do not accept or reject on the call. Say you would like to consider it, ask for the full package details, and follow up.

### Location / relocation

**30 seconds.** "I'm based in [city] and open to [cities/remote]."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then one sentence on your constraints (visa, family, current lease).

**120 seconds.** Same opener, then a sentence on what the right answer looks like for you.

### Work authorisation

**30 seconds.** "I'm authorised to work in [country] on [visa status] through [date]. [Sponsorship needs now / in the future / not needed]."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then one sentence on any related details (OPT end date, STEM extension pending, etc.).

**120 seconds.** Same opener, then one sentence on your longer-term plans if relevant.

### Start date

**30 seconds.** "I can start [date or 'immediately' or 'after graduation on date']."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then one sentence on any flexibility.

### Other interviews / offers

**30 seconds.** "I'm in process with [count or 'a few'] other roles at similar stage. I'm prioritising [criteria]."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then one sentence on how you are evaluating them.

### Why are you looking?

**30 seconds.** "I'm looking for [specific outcome]. My current situation is [specifics: graduating, completing an internship, finishing a contract]."

**60 seconds.** Same opener, then one sentence on what you want next and what you do not.

### Questions for me

Have three. Rotate based on the conversation. Use [Questions before accepting](../questions-before-accepting/) as a source for the offer-stage version.

- "What does the team look like today, and how is it expected to grow?"
- "What does the first three months look like for someone in this role?"
- "How does the company think about [specific thing from your research]?"
- "What's something the team has shipped recently that you're proud of?"

## Practice the format, not just the content

Rehearse out loud. The 30/60/120-second versions are a skill you build by saying them, not by reading them.

- Use a timer. Check whether your answer is concise enough for the question and available time.
- Record yourself. Listening back catches filler words ("um", "like", "so") and trailing sentences.
- Practice with a peer or a model. A model can run a mock recruiter screen; a peer can give you the social cues. See [The AI Job Search Framework](../ai-job-search-framework/) section D.

## What you should not do

- Confirm whether the screen includes domain or technical questions, and prepare proportionately.
- Do not lie. The recruiter will find out. Describe offers and deadlines accurately. You can keep employer names and confidential documents private.
- Do not ask about salary before you have been told the role. Ask early in the conversation, after role fit is established.
- Do not badmouth a previous employer. The recruiter will assume you will badmouth this one too.
- Do not accept the role on the call. The recruiter is selling; you should be deciding.

## The recruiter screen and the rest of the loop

The recruiter screen sets the tone for the loop. Two things follow it.

- **Technical rounds.** See [Coding interviews](../coding-interviews/), [Behavioral interviews](../behavioral-interviews/), [System design](../system-design/) and the relevant preparation guide.
- **Hiring-manager call.** Often the last screen before the on-site. See [How to prepare for a multi-round interview loop](../how-to-prepare-for-a-multi-round-interview-loop/).

The recruiter screen is also where you learn what the rest of the loop looks like. Ask.
