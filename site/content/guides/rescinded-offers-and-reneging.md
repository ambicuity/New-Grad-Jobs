An accepted offer can still change. Sometimes the employer withdraws it or pushes the start date back; sometimes your own circumstances change. This guide covers both directions. The core idea is the same either way: get the facts in writing, act promptly, and tell the people who need to know. It does not replace advice from an employment attorney, your school's career center or, for F-1 students, your DSO.

## If your offer is rescinded or delayed

Do these in order, starting the day you hear:

1. **Get it in writing.** If the news came by phone, reply by email: "Thank you for letting me know. Could you confirm in writing that the offer for [role] dated [date] has been withdrawn [or: that my start date has moved to (date)], and the reason?" NACE's [advisory opinion on rescinded and deferred offers](https://naceweb.org/career-development/organizational-structure/advisory-opinion-rescinded-and-deferred-employment-offers/) recommends that employers give "a written statement that the offer has been revoked."
2. **Ask about timing.** For a delay: is the new date firm, will it be confirmed in writing, and is the offer otherwise unchanged? For a withdrawal: is there a chance of a later start date or a different role?
3. **Ask about money you have spent or been paid.** Relocation you incurred, a signing bonus already paid, a lease you signed. Ask whether the employer will reimburse costs or waive any repayment term. NACE suggests employers consider "reimbursing the candidate for expenses incurred in the acceptance of employment, such as travel and relocation expenses," and short-term financial assistance or services to help them secure other employment. That is a professional association's recommendation, not a legal requirement.
4. **Ask about severance only if it applies.** The US Department of Labor says "there is no requirement in the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) for severance pay"; it is "a matter of agreement" between employer and employee ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/wages/severancepay)). If your agreement or the employer's policy mentions it, ask.
5. **Restart the search the same day.** Reopen anything you withdrew from with a short note: "I withdrew earlier because I had accepted another offer. That offer has since been withdrawn, and I'm still very interested in [role]. Is it still open?" Then check [new this week](../../jobs/new-this-week/) and [Searching after graduation](../searching-after-graduation/).
6. **Tell your career center.** NACE's opinion says career services should "assist students in their search for employment" and "advocate and mediate an equitable resolution." It also says career services should address ongoing relations with employers that have a pattern of deferring or rescinding offers, so your report can help the next student.

Keep every email, the offer letter and any receipts in one folder. If you think the reason given was discriminatory, an employment attorney or the agency in your jurisdiction can tell you your options and deadlines.

## F-1 students: tell your DSO now

If you are on post-completion OPT, a withdrawn or delayed offer can affect your unemployment count. NACE's opinion says international students "should speak with their career center and office of international affairs regarding options." Report the change as your DSO directs, and read [OPT unemployment days](../opt-unemployment-days/) for the limits and the records to keep. If you were relying on a STEM extension or an H-1B petition from this employer, ask your DSO and the employer's immigration counsel what the withdrawal means for those filings.

## Unemployment insurance

Whether you can claim unemployment benefits depends on the state, and on your own work history. The Department of Labor says requirements generally include wage or work requirements over a set period, which matters if you have not yet started a job.

::: evidence official
The [US Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/unemployment-insurance) states that "each state administers a separate unemployment insurance program" and "each state sets its own unemployment insurance benefits eligibility guidelines," and that you file with the program in the state where you worked. CareerOneStop's [Unemployment Benefits Finder](https://www.careeronestop.org/LocalHelp/UnemploymentBenefits/find-unemployment-benefits.aspx), sponsored by the Department of Labor, links to each state's filing page.
:::

Check your state's own page rather than a summary. Outside the US, use the official programme for your province or country.

## If you are thinking of reneging

Reneging means backing out of an offer you already accepted. Before you decide:

- **Reread what you signed.** Look for a start-date commitment, a signing-bonus or relocation repayment clause, and anything about notice. [Reading your employment agreement](../reading-your-employment-agreement/) covers what to look for. Legal obligations depend on the document and the jurisdiction; ask an attorney if you are unsure.
- **Check your school's policy.** Many career centers have one, and it can apply even if the employer does not object. Two examples:

| School | What the policy says |
| --- | --- |
| [UNC-Chapel Hill](https://policies.unc.edu/TDClient/2833/Portal/KB/ArticleDet?ID=135374) | Calls reneging "the most serious unprofessional recruiting behavior"; recruiting privileges are suspended until the student meets the External Relations Director; the maximum penalty for a full-time offer is no Handshake job applications or alumni services for up to 12 months after graduation. The policy is part of UNC's on-campus recruiting rules. |
| [Texas A&M](https://careercenter.tamu.edu/current-students/interviews-and-offers/) | Students agree to stop interviewing once they accept; a student who reneges "will be blocked from using HireAggies" and their department head is notified. It also asks employers to refrain from withdrawing offers. |

These are examples only. Read your own school's policy, and if you are unsure, ask the career center before you act.

- **Check visa consequences.** If the accepted job is tied to your OPT reporting or a STEM I-983, talk to your DSO first.
- **Decide quickly.** The longer you wait, the less time the employer has to fill the role, and the closer you get to a start date people are planning around.

## How to renege, if you decide to

Call or email the recruiter as soon as you have decided, then confirm in writing:

> **Subject:** [Your name], [role] offer
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I'm writing to let you know that I won't be able to join [company] as [role]. [One honest sentence, for example: "My circumstances have changed and I've decided to accept a different position."] I know this creates work for your team, and I'm sorry for that. I wanted to tell you as soon as I had decided.
>
> If there is anything I need to return or repay under my offer, please let me know and I will take care of it.
>
> Thank you for your time and for the offer.
>
> [Your name]

Do not invent a reason, and do not go silent. Tell your career center, as their policy may require. Repay anything your agreement requires.
