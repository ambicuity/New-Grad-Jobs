Networking for new grads is not a personality trait; it is a small weekly system. Conversations can help you understand roles and build professional relationships alongside direct applications. There is no universal share of new-grad hires attributable to networking.

## A workable definition

One optional routine is sending a few short, specific messages a week to people you have a real connection to: alumni, former internship colleagues, classmates who graduated a year or two ahead, recruiters at target companies, and people who post about your target teams. The goal is to learn one thing per conversation and to be remembered when a role opens.

## The weekly loop

1. **Monday: pick the targets.** From your [target-company list](../build-a-target-company-list/), name the three companies you most want to hear from this week.
2. **Tuesday to Thursday: send them.** Alumni first (warmest), then recruiters, then hiring managers or people at the team level. One message per person, no mass DMs.
3. **Friday: follow up.** Reply to anyone who responded, file the rest under "try again next month".
4. **Weekend: write down one thing you learned.** Keep a single file or spreadsheet so you do not have to ask the same question twice.

## What to send

A short note that says who you are, what you want, and why you picked this person specifically. No résumé attached, no "can I pick your brain", no multiple paragraphs. Three to five sentences.

## What you do not need

You do not need a large following, a polished personal brand, or anyone to "open doors" for you. You do not need to attend conferences, post on LinkedIn daily, or pitch yourself to strangers. You do need to send the messages.

## When it stops being enough

A month of outreach without referrals does not establish a failure. Review whether your contacts and questions are relevant, and value useful information as well as referrals. Read [Diagnose your job-search funnel](../diagnose-your-job-search-funnel/) before changing tactics.
