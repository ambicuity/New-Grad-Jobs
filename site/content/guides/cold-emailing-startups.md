At a company of a few dozen people, the founder or the person who will manage you may also read applications. A short, specific email to that person can put your evidence in front of them alongside your application. It is not a shortcut around the posted process, and it does not oblige anyone to reply. This guide is about the email itself; for recruiters, referrals and wider networking, see the related guides linked below.

## When a cold email makes sense

Write when at least one of these is true:

- **There is a posted role** and you have applied, or will apply, through the company's own careers page.
- **There is a clear, visible need** that matches your work: a job post on their site, a public request for help, a product launch that needs exactly what you have done.
- **The company is small enough** that the founder or a team lead plausibly reads candidate email. At larger employers, use [How to message recruiters](../messaging-recruiters/) instead.

Do not write to ask "are you hiring?" with nothing attached. If the company has a careers page that says "no openings", respect it. You can note the company on your [target-company list](../build-a-target-company-list/) and check back later.

If you are still deciding whether a small company suits you, read [Startup vs enterprise](../startup-vs-enterprise/) first.

## Find the right person

Pick one person. Writing to three founders and two engineers at a ten-person company with the same message reads as spam to all five.

1. **The posting.** Some job posts name the hiring manager or say who the role reports to.
2. **The company's own pages.** Team, about or blog pages often show who leads the function you would join.
3. **Their public writing.** A founder's post about hiring, or an engineer's write-up of a project, tells you who cares about the area.
4. **The right address.** Use an address the company or person publishes for this purpose, a careers address, or a work address listed on the company site. Do not send to a personal address you dug up, and do not message someone on several channels at once.

Choose the hiring manager for the role over the CEO when you can tell who that is. In a company with a handful of people, the founder may be the hiring manager.

## What to send

Keep it short enough to read on a phone. State who you are, which role, one piece of evidence and a small request.

::: evidence practical
Loyola University New Orleans' career center advises that a cold email [should be no more than a few short paragraphs, with a short, specific subject line and the purpose clear in the first paragraph](https://career.loyno.edu/students/job-internship-search/email-templates), and that you can share your résumé, a streamlined portfolio or a link to your website.
:::

> **Subject:** [Role title] application: [your name], [one-phrase credential]
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> I applied for the [role title] on your careers page today. I'm a [degree] graduate from [school], and I recently [one specific thing you did that matches the role, with a result you can show].
>
> The part of the role I'd be most useful on is [specific responsibility from the posting], because [one sentence of evidence]. Here is [the work]: [link].
>
> If it would help, I'm happy to answer any questions in a short call. Either way, thank you for reading.
>
> [Full name] · [LinkedIn or portfolio link] · [city, time zone]

Examples of the evidence line by field:

- **Operations:** "I rebuilt the inventory tracking for a campus food pantry, cutting the weekly count from [hours] to [hours]. The before-and-after process is in this two-page write-up."
- **Marketing:** "I ran the newsletter for [student organization] and grew it from [number] to [number] subscribers over a semester; here is the campaign log."
- **Software:** "I built [tool] that does [thing] with [stack]; the repo and a short screen recording are here."

Use only numbers you can show. If you have none, describe the work and link it. The [résumé evidence test](../resume-evidence-test/) applies to emails too.

## What evidence to include

- **One link, maybe two.** A live demo, a write-up, a portfolio piece, a merged pull request or a short case study. Link it rather than attaching large files.
- **Something they can check in under a few minutes.** A landing page for a long project is better than a raw folder.
- **Your work, clearly marked.** If a project was a team effort, say what your part was.
- **Your résumé as a link or a single PDF** if the company's process does not already have it.

See [Personal websites and live demos](../personal-websites-and-live-demos/) for making a demo that still works when someone clicks it.

## Follow up once

1. Wait about a week. Small teams are busy and a quiet inbox means nothing about you.
2. Reply in the same thread with one or two sentences: "Following up on my note below about the [role]. Happy to send anything else that's useful." Add one new piece of information if you have it.
3. Stop there. If there is no reply after the follow-up, the application through the careers page still stands. Do not switch to their personal social accounts.

## Respecting no

A "no", "not right now" or "please apply through the site" is a complete answer.

> Thanks for letting me know, [name]. I appreciate the reply. If the team's needs change I'd be glad to hear from you, and I'll keep following [company]'s work.

Then stop writing to that person about that role. Do not argue, ask them to reconsider or forward the email to a colleague. If they ask you not to contact them, do not. For later, polite contact after a rejection, see [Email templates after a rejection](../email-templates-after-rejection/).
