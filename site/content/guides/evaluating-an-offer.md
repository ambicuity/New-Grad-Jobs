The first offer is the hardest to judge because there is nothing to hold it against, and relief makes everything look good. Judge it on its own terms first. If a second offer arrives, [Comparing offers](../comparing-offers/) builds on this.

## Get it in writing

An offer is a letter with a base salary, any bonus and equity, a start date, a location, a title and level, and a deadline. Verbal numbers are intentions. If the recruiter has only called, ask for the letter before you evaluate anything.

## Four questions

**1. Does the money work?** Compute take-home after tax and housing in that city, and check it against your actual costs, including loan payments. This is a yes or no, not a ranking. [Understanding compensation](../understanding-compensation/) explains the parts; count base, cash-equivalent benefits and a discounted bonus, and count private equity at zero for this question.

**2. Will you learn?** The first job sets the pace of the next five years. Signs of a good learning environment: a named manager who has managed new grads, a team of more than a few people, code review or its equivalent in your field, an onboarding plan, and work that ships. Signs of a poor one: you would be the only person in your role, the manager is undefined, or the answer to "what will I work on" was vague. You learn from these things, not from the company's name.

**3. Do you like the people?** You met four or five of them in the loop. Would you ask them for help? Did they seem to like their work? Was the hiring manager clear and honest? This is not soft; it predicts whether you will still be there in a year.

**4. What is the risk?** For a large employer: reorganizations and whether the team's project is central or peripheral. For a startup: runway, and what happens to you if it ends. Ask directly; a good employer will answer. Add your own: visa timelines, a partner's location, a graduate program you might defer.

## Weighing them

Money must clear the bar; beyond the bar, weight learning highest for a first job. A first role that teaches a lot at a slightly lower salary compounds; the reverse does not. People and risk are tie-breakers unless one is alarming, in which case it is a veto.

## The deadline

Deadlines are real but usually movable by a week or two, especially when you are mid-loop elsewhere. Ask politely, once, with a reason. An employer that refuses any extension for a new grad is telling you something.

## Red flags

- Pressure to accept on the call
- A title or level that does not match the posting
- Refusal to put numbers in writing
- Equity described only in dollars, never in units and schedule
- Vagueness about the team, the manager or the work

None of these is necessarily fatal, but each is a question to ask before you sign. [Questions before accepting](../questions-before-accepting/) has the list.

## Accepting

When you accept, tell every other employer in your pipeline the same day, briefly and gratefully. Reneging on an accepted offer is remembered; keep looking only until you accept, then stop.
