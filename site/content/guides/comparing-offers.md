Two offers feel like a luxury and behave like a problem: they arrive on different dates with different structures and different deadlines. The method is to normalize them, then compare on the things the numbers do not capture, then decide once.

## Normalize the money

Build one table with a row per offer and these columns:

- Base salary
- Signing bonus, divided by the years of its clawback
- Annual bonus target, multiplied by what the company actually paid recently
- Equity per year of vesting, at the current price for a public company and at a heavy discount, or zero, for a private one
- Retirement match and other cash-equivalent benefits
- Estimated tax and housing for the location, subtracted

The last row is what each offer leaves you per year in that city. Do the arithmetic; the gap is often smaller or larger than the headline suggested. [Understanding compensation](../understanding-compensation/) explains each column.

## Then compare what the table cannot hold

Score each offer on the four questions from [Evaluating an offer](../evaluating-an-offer/): money clears the bar, learning, people, risk. Add anything specific to you: proximity to family, visa position, remote policy, the industry you want to be in. Write it down. Seeing "learning: strong / weak" next to "money: +10%" makes the trade explicit.

## Align the deadlines

Tell each employer, honestly, that you have another offer and its deadline. Ask the earlier one for an extension and the later one to accelerate. Most will move; cohort programs at large employers sometimes cannot, and then you must decide with what you have. Do not invent offers. Recruiters talk, and the industry is small.

## Use one to improve the other

A competing offer is the strongest lever in [Negotiation](../negotiation/). Use it on the offer you prefer, not on both: ask the employer you want whether they can close the gap, share the real figure, and accept if they do. Playing the two against each other for weeks damages both relationships and rarely yields more than the first counter.

## Deciding

Prefer the offer that is better on learning and not worse than the bar on money. Prefer the offer whose people you would rather ask for help. If everything is close, prefer the one where the manager was clearest about what you would do in the first three months; clarity at the offer stage predicts clarity on the job.

Then decide once, tell both employers the same day, and stop comparing. The worst outcome is not choosing the wrong offer; it is spending the first month at the new job still weighing the other one.

## A note on names

A well-known employer on the résumé helps with the second job. It is a real factor and it belongs in the "risk" column as a reduction. It is not worth a poor manager or a role with nothing to learn; a strong first year anywhere reads better than a weak one anywhere.
