Some employers ask about grades and some never do. When a posting or application does ask, the only safe approach is to give the number your school records, labelled so a reader cannot mistake it for something else. This guide covers reading a stated requirement, presenting your GPA, transcript requests and explaining an uneven academic record.

## Read the requirement before deciding

A GPA line in a posting can be a hard screen, a preference or a regional default. Read the exact wording and where it appears.

- **"Required", "must have" or an eligibility section.** Treat it as a firm condition. If you are below it, you do not meet the stated criteria. Check whether the posting or employer names an alternative route, such as another program or a different qualification basis.
- **"Preferred" or "ideally".** It is a preference, not a rule. Decide using the rest of the posting, as in [Are you qualified?](../are-you-qualified/)
- **No GPA mentioned.** Do not assume a hidden cutoff exists, and do not assume one does not. Answer what the application actually asks.

Requirements can differ by location and program within one employer. For example, [BNY's analyst program page](https://bny.com/corporate/global/en/careers/students/analyst-program.html) says academic requirements vary by region and program and asks applicants to meet the minimum GPA, degree classification or equivalent for the location they apply to. Check the posting for your country rather than a number you saw for another office.

::: evidence official
US federal hiring publishes its rules. OPM's [General Schedule qualification policies](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/classification-qualifications/general-schedule-qualification-policies/) define Superior Academic Achievement for GS-7 entry: upper third of the class, a 3.0 or higher GPA (all four years or the final two), a 3.5 or higher in major-field courses, or membership in a recognised national honor society. GPAs are rounded to one decimal place and taken from, or computed from, the final transcript covering the period used.
:::

That rule applies to US federal positions where the qualification standard uses it. It is an example of a published standard, not a benchmark for private employers. Read the specific announcement and qualification standard for any federal role.

## Decide whether to list your GPA

Listing a GPA is a choice when no one asks for it, and a requirement when an application does. A reasonable approach:

1. If the posting or application asks for GPA, provide it in the requested format.
2. If it does not ask, include it when it supports your application and leave it off when it does not. Leaving it off is not misrepresentation. Writing a different number is.
3. As you add relevant work history, you can give the education section less space. Keep it accurate either way.

## Label it so it cannot mislead

A reader who sees "GPA: 3.6" will assume it is your cumulative GPA on your school's scale. If it is anything else, say so.

| Situation | Honest format |
|---|---|
| Cumulative GPA | `GPA: 3.42/4.00` |
| Major GPA is stronger and relevant | `Major GPA: 3.71/4.00 (cumulative 3.18/4.00)` or `Major GPA: 3.71/4.00` with the label kept |
| Non-4.0 scale, for example a 10-point CGPA | `CGPA: 8.1/10` with the scale shown |
| Degree classification | `First Class Honours` or `Upper Second (2:1)`, as awarded |
| Graduate degree | List the graduate GPA separately from the undergraduate one |

Use the figure your registrar reports; if you shorten it, use standard rounding only (3.42 becomes 3.4). Duke Economics' [recruiting FAQ](https://econ.duke.edu/recruiting-faqs) says rounding a 3.43 GPA to 3.5 is not acceptable and may be treated as academic dishonesty. Do not convert a non-US grade to a 4.0 scale yourself unless the application asks you to and explains how; if it asks for a converted figure, state the method or use a credential evaluation if one is requested.

A major GPA is a calculation over particular courses. Compute it from your transcript and the courses your program counts as the major, and be ready to show the working.

## Answer application questions truthfully

Some application forms have a required GPA field. Enter exactly what it asks for: cumulative if it says cumulative, on the scale it specifies. If the form has no space for context, the cover letter or an "additional information" box can carry one or two sentences. See [Cover letters and application questions](../cover-letters-and-application-questions/).

Short wording you can adapt:

> My cumulative GPA is 2.9/4.0. My GPA in the final two years, in my major courses, is 3.4/4.0, and the transcript shows the change after my second year.

> My university awards degree classifications rather than a GPA. I graduated with an Upper Second (2:1).

Do not describe a figure that does not appear on, or cannot be calculated from, your transcript.

## Transcript requests

An employer may ask for an unofficial transcript early and an official one later, or ask only after an offer. Ask which version they need and how it should be sent.

- **Unofficial transcript:** usually a copy you download from your student portal. Send it unaltered.
- **Official transcript:** issued by your registrar, sometimes sent directly to the employer. Allow time for processing and any fees.
- **Not graduated yet:** send the in-progress transcript and say when the final one will be available. Federal rules, for example, let agencies appoint senior students pending verification of final grades and warn that an applicant who does not keep the required grades may not be able to keep the GS-7 grade or the position.
- **Education outside the country:** ask whether a credential evaluation or translation is required and who pays for it.

Pre-employment checks can include degree and date verification. See [Background checks and pre-employment verification](../background-checks-and-pre-employment-verification/) for what to expect and how to fix errors in records.

## Gaps, withdrawals and repeated courses

A withdrawal, a leave of absence, a repeated course or a part-time semester appears on many transcripts. You do not owe anyone your medical or personal history, but do not hide dates that a verification will show.

- **On the résumé:** list your actual enrolment and graduation dates. A gap does not need a line of explanation.
- **If asked in an interview or form:** one sentence, then move on. "I took a medical leave in 2024 and returned full-time the next term." or "I worked full-time for a year to fund my studies."
- **Repeated or replaced grades:** report your GPA as your school calculates it. If its policy replaces a grade, the recalculated figure is your GPA.
- **Academic probation or dismissal:** answer direct questions accurately. Explain what changed and point to your later results.

If a disability affected your record, accommodations during hiring are covered in [Interview accessibility and accommodations](../interview-accessibility-and-accommodations/).
