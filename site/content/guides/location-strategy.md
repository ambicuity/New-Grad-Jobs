Where you are willing to work shapes your search as much as which role you want. A wider area gives you more postings to choose from; a narrower one lets you know employers well and avoid moves you cannot afford. This guide helps you decide deliberately: which areas to include, how to read remote and hybrid terms, what to ask about relocation, and how to compare costs with official data rather than impressions.

## Decide your search area first

Write down your constraints before you look at postings, so the postings do not decide for you:

- **Where you can live.** Family, health, a partner's job, a lease, or the cost of a move.
- **Where you can legally work.** Citizenship, visa or permit terms may limit country or employer; see [Canada and India job search](../canada-and-india-job-search/) if you are searching outside the US.
- **How you will get to work.** A car, transit, or neither changes what "commutable" means.
- **What you can afford to start.** A deposit, first month's rent and travel before the first paycheck.

Then sort places into three groups: where you will work without hesitation, where you would move for a strong role, and where you will not go. Apply only to the first two. [Build a target-company list](../build-a-target-company-list/) uses the same tiering for employers.

## Metros and regional employers

A large metro can put many employers within one commute, which can mean more postings to choose from and more options to change jobs later without moving. It can also mean higher costs and more competition for each opening; check both for the places you are considering.

Regional employers can be easy to miss, because some recruit less on campus or advertise less widely. Hospital systems, utilities, manufacturers, banks and credit unions, school districts, state and local governments, and logistics companies can all hire entry-level staff outside the largest cities. To find them, search the employer's own careers page, your state or provincial government's job site, and local professional associations. The board covers configured employers and sources, so a company absent from it may still be hiring.

## Remote, hybrid and on-site

Read the work arrangement in the posting, not the headline. Terms vary:

- **On-site** means you work at the location named.
- **Hybrid** usually means set days in the office. Ask how many and whether they are fixed.
- **Remote** may still be limited to certain states, provinces or countries where the employer can hire and run payroll, or may require you to live near an office. Look for phrases such as "remote, US only" or a list of eligible states.

For a first job, ask how training and mentoring work in each arrangement. Questions such as "How does a new hire get feedback in their first months?" and "How often does the team meet in person?" tell you more than the label does. [Questions before accepting](../questions-before-accepting/) has more.

## Compare costs with official data

Compare pay against what living there costs, using sources that state their method. Keep the year and geography beside every figure.

::: evidence official
BEA's [regional price parities](https://www.bea.gov/data/prices-inflation/regional-price-parities-state-and-metro-area) measure price-level differences across states and metro areas as a percentage of the overall national price level. A value above the national level means prices are higher than average there.
:::

Use RPPs to compare two offers or two areas in broad terms, not to predict your own rent or budget. Check current local rents directly for the neighborhoods you would actually live in.

For pay by place, [How to research your market salary](../compensation-research/) explains the US Bureau of Labor Statistics wage estimates by state and metro, and Canada's Job Bank [wage search](https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/trend-analysis/search-wages) lets you compare wages by occupation and by city or postal code. These figures cover workers at every experience level, so they are context, not your expected offer; compare them with posted ranges for the role.

## Ask about relocation before you accept

If a role needs a move, ask in writing, ideally before you accept:

1. Is relocation assistance offered for this role? Is it a lump sum, reimbursement, or a service the employer arranges?
2. What does it cover: travel, moving goods, temporary housing, a lease break?
3. Is any of it repayable if I leave within a stated period?
4. When is it paid: before the move, or after I submit receipts?
5. Can the start date allow time to find housing?

Ask payroll or a tax professional how relocation payments will be treated for tax. In the US, the IRS's [moving expenses topic](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc455) describes the moving-expense deduction for active-duty Armed Forces members moving under a military order and, for moves in 2026 or later, certain intelligence community employees, so most civilian employees should not plan on deducting a move. [Reading your employment agreement](../reading-your-employment-agreement/) covers repayment clauses.

## Filter by location on the board

The board has three location tools, and they combine with the role and other filters:

- **COUNTRY** narrows to the United States, Canada or India.
- **LOCATION** lists the metros with the most current postings. Choose one or several.
- **REMOTE** has remote, hybrid and onsite chips. The board reads these from the words "remote" and "hybrid" in a posting's location and title. A posting with neither word is shown as onsite, so check the posting itself before relying on the label.

For a smaller city that is not in the LOCATION list, type its name or state in the search box, which matches location text. The remote [feed](../how-to-use-this-board/) lets you get new remote postings as they appear.
