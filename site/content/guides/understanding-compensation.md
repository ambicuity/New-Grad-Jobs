A new grad offer is several numbers dressed as one. Employers present "total compensation" because it is the largest figure, but the parts behave differently, and the differences matter more at the start of a career than at any other time. This guide names the parts; [Evaluating an offer](../evaluating-an-offer/) covers deciding.

## Base salary

The fixed amount paid while you are employed under the agreed terms. Use it as the starting point for a budget, after taxes and deductions. Ask how pay reviews work; neither a raise nor a particular percentage is guaranteed. Do not assume a future employer can ask for salary history: some jurisdictions prohibit it, including [New York](https://www.ny.gov/salary-history-ban/salary-history-ban-what-you-need-know). Use the role’s market range and your expectations in pay discussions.

## Signing bonus

A one-time payment, usually in the first pay period, and usually with a clawback if you leave within a year or two. It is real money but it does not recur, so a larger signing bonus with a lower base is worse than it looks over any period longer than a year. Signing bonuses are also the part employers move most easily; see [Negotiation](../negotiation/).

## Annual bonus

A target percentage of base, paid once a year, and dependent on company and individual performance. Ask what the target is, what the company paid last year against target, and whether new hires in the first year are prorated or excluded. Last year’s payout is evidence of variability, not a promise of this year’s result. Compare a base-only budget with separate target and lower-payout scenarios.

## Equity

Shares, restricted stock units or options can vest over different schedules; read the actual grant terms rather than assuming a four-year schedule or a one-year cliff. At a public company, a grant is valued at the current share price and behaves like deferred cash that goes up and down. At a private company it is options or units whose value depends on a future sale or listing that may not happen. Ask, at minimum: the number of units, the vesting schedule, the most recent valuation and how it was set, and for options the strike price and the post-termination exercise window. Do not count private equity as salary when planning your life.

## Benefits

Health insurance and what you pay for it, retirement matching, paid leave, relocation, a learning budget, equipment. Retirement contributions vary, and employer contributions may have their own eligibility and vesting requirements. Add the ones that are cash-equivalent to your comparison; note the rest.

## Location and cost of living

The same base means different things in different cities, and remote roles at some employers pay by location. Compare after housing, tax and commute, not before.

## Reading a posted range

Some postings include a salary range, often because a jurisdiction requires it. The board shows it when it is posted and can sort by it. Ask which location, level, pay period and currency the range covers and whether it is base or total compensation. A posted midpoint is not a negotiation ceiling, and a broad range does not identify your applicable level. Some laws require a good-faith expected range; see [New York’s pay-transparency guidance](https://dol.ny.gov/pay-transparency).

## Compare country-specific terms

Compare U.S. and Canadian offers in their stated currencies and distinguish employer benefits from statutory deductions. For Indian offers, ask for the full CTC breakdown and identify fixed gross cash, variable pay, employer contributions, gratuity-related amounts and one-time payments. CTC is not monthly take-home pay. Request a sample payroll calculation using the applicable tax assumptions.

## What is not in the offer letter

Promotion timing, raise cycles, how bonuses are actually decided, how equity refreshes work. These decide what the job pays in year three. [Questions before accepting](../questions-before-accepting/) lists what to ask.
