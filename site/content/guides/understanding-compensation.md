A new grad offer is several numbers dressed as one. Employers present "total compensation" because it is the largest figure, but the parts behave differently, and the differences matter more at the start of a career than at any other time. This guide names the parts; [Evaluating an offer](../evaluating-an-offer/) covers deciding.

## Base salary

The fixed amount paid through the year. It is the number that rent, loans and savings are planned around, it is the number future raises are a percentage of, and it is the number the next employer will ask about. Everything else is conditional; base is not. Weight it accordingly.

## Signing bonus

A one-time payment, usually in the first pay period, and usually with a clawback if you leave within a year or two. It is real money but it does not recur, so a larger signing bonus with a lower base is worse than it looks over any period longer than a year. Signing bonuses are also the part employers move most easily; see [Negotiation](../negotiation/).

## Annual bonus

A target percentage of base, paid once a year, and dependent on company and individual performance. Ask what the target is, what the company paid last year against target, and whether new hires in the first year are prorated or excluded. A "15% target" that paid 8% last year is an 8% bonus.

## Equity

Shares, or the right to buy shares, vesting over a schedule, typically four years with a cliff at one. At a public company, a grant is valued at the current share price and behaves like deferred cash that goes up and down. At a private company it is options or units whose value depends on a future sale or listing that may not happen. Ask, at minimum: the number of units, the vesting schedule, the most recent valuation and how it was set, and for options the strike price and the post-termination exercise window. Do not count private equity as salary when planning your life.

## Benefits

Health insurance and what you pay for it, retirement matching, paid leave, relocation, a learning budget, equipment. Retirement matching in particular is straightforward money and varies widely. Add the ones that are cash-equivalent to your comparison; note the rest.

## Location and cost of living

The same base means different things in different cities, and remote roles at some employers pay by location. Compare after housing, tax and commute, not before.

## Reading a posted range

Some postings include a salary range, often because a jurisdiction requires it. The board shows it when it is posted and can sort by it. Ranges are wide because they cover several levels or locations; a new grad offer usually lands in the lower half unless the range is level-specific. Treat the bottom of the range as the floor for the level, and the middle as the ceiling to aim for in negotiation.

## What is not in the offer letter

Promotion timing, raise cycles, how bonuses are actually decided, how equity refreshes work. These decide what the job pays in year three. [Questions before accepting](../questions-before-accepting/) lists what to ask.
