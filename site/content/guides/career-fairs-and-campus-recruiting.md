A career fair is a short conversation with someone who may influence an employer's early recruiting. It rarely produces an offer on the day; the useful outcomes are an accurate picture of the openings, a named contact and a clear next step. This guide works for any field, from accounting to nursing to civil engineering.

::: evidence practical
MIT's career office notes that [it is not typical to come away from a career fair with a job offer](https://capd.mit.edu/tips-for-career-fair-success-for-students/); new contacts and invitations to apply or interview are realistic goals. It also advises choosing your top employers in advance, because you are unlikely to have time to speak to all of them.
:::

## Before the fair: build a short target list

Many fairs publish the list of registered employers in advance, often in your school's career platform. Use it to plan, not to tour every table.

1. Download or copy the employer list.
2. Mark the employers that hire for your field and work location. Check each one's careers page for current entry-level openings and the degree or authorization requirements they state.
3. Pick a first group you must reach and a second group for if time allows. Put the first group in the order you will visit, starting with one lower-pressure conversation to warm up.
4. For each target, write down one specific fact (a program, a location, a product, a client type) and one question the website does not answer.
5. Note which roles you will name. "I'm interested in your [program or role title]" is more useful to a recruiter than "What do you have?"

If you already keep a [target-company list](../build-a-target-company-list/), add fair employers to it so follow-up lives in one place.

## What to bring

- Printed résumés, more than you expect to need. UMass's career guide notes [there is usually nowhere to make copies at the fair](https://www.umass.edu/humanities-arts/careers/career-guides/career-fair-prep). Tailor a version for each field you are targeting.
- A folder, a pen and a notepad, or a notes app you can use quickly.
- Your target list with the facts and questions for each employer.
- Your student ID or event registration, if the fair requires it.
- Clothes appropriate to the event and your field. If unsure, ask your career center what employers at that fair expect.

## What to say: a short introduction

Aim for a 30–60 second introduction that ends with a question. MIT suggests [a 30–60 second pitch](https://capd.mit.edu/tips-for-career-fair-success-for-students/); UMass suggests something closer to 15 seconds. Pick the length that lets you sound natural.

> Hi, I'm [name], a [degree and major] student graduating in [month, year]. I'm interested in your [role or program], because [one specific reason tied to the fact you found]. Recently I [one relevant project, job or result]. What does the team look for in new graduates applying to that role?

Variations:

- **Nursing:** "I finished my clinical rotation in [unit] and I'm interested in your new graduate residency at [hospital]. When does the next cohort open?"
- **Finance:** "I'm a [major] graduating in May. I saw your [rotational analyst program]. Which locations are hiring this cycle?"
- **Software or data:** "I built [project] using [tools]. Does your [team] hire new grads directly, or mainly through internships?"

Then listen. Useful questions: How do new graduates apply? Is there a deadline or a specific requisition number? What happens after the fair? Who should I follow up with? If the answer is "apply online", that is normal. Ask for the role name or link and a contact for follow-up.

Write two lines of notes as soon as you step away: the person's name, what they said, and the next step.

## Info sessions and other campus recruiting

Employers also recruit through information sessions, workshops, class visits and on-campus interviews arranged through the career center.

- **Info sessions:** Arrive on time, ask one prepared question, and introduce yourself briefly afterward if there is time. Note the presenter's name and any deadline mentioned.
- **On-campus interviews:** These usually require applying through your school's platform by a set date. Read the eligibility rules and deadlines carefully.
- **Career center appointments:** Ask an adviser which employers recruit your major and what deadlines your field follows. See [The new grad hiring timeline](../new-grad-hiring-timeline/) for how timing differs.

## Following up

Send a short email within a day or two while you both remember the conversation. UMass suggests [a short thank-you email within 24 hours](https://www.umass.edu/humanities-arts/careers/career-guides/career-fair-prep).

> **Subject:** [Fair name] follow-up: [your name], [role]
>
> Hi [name], thank you for speaking with me at [fair name] about [role or program]. I appreciated hearing about [specific point]. I have applied through your careers site (requisition [number]) and attached my résumé. Please let me know if there is anything else I should send.
>
> Best, [name], [phone or LinkedIn]

Apply through the official channel before or alongside the email. If you have no reply after a week or so, one short follow-up is enough. For later messages to recruiters, use [How to message recruiters](../messaging-recruiters/).

## Virtual fairs

Virtual fairs often split time into group sessions and short one-to-one sessions. At the University of Washington, for example, [group sessions run 30 minutes with up to 75 participants and one-to-one sessions run 15 minutes](https://careers.uw.edu/virtual-career-fairs/), with slots allocated first-come, first-served. Formats vary by school and platform, so read your fair's instructions.

- Register for the fair and book sessions as soon as signups open.
- Complete your platform profile and upload a current résumé. Employers may see it when they download a session participant list.
- Test your camera, microphone and connection beforehand. Use a quiet space and a laptop or desktop if the platform recommends one.
- Keep your introduction shorter than in person. In a group session, ask one specific question in the chat.

## If your school brings few employers

A small fair does not limit where you can apply. Some alternatives:

- **Ask your career center** whether it belongs to a consortium fair, shares fairs with nearby schools, or offers alumni access to other events.
- **Look for field-specific fairs** run by professional associations, licensing bodies or hospital systems. Check each event's eligibility; some are open beyond a single school.
- **Use public workforce services.** In the US, the Department of Labor describes [American Job Centers](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/american-job-centers) as offering job search help to job seekers and recruitment help to employers, including arranging job fairs.
- **Apply directly.** Employers recruit through their own careers sites as well as campus events, so a missing table does not mean a missing opening. Browse [entry-level jobs on the board](../../jobs/) and see [Job search without an internship](../job-search-without-an-internship/).
- **Contact people instead of tables.** Use alumni and the routine in [How to network as a new grad](../networking-as-a-new-grad/).

Verify any employer you meet outside your school before sharing personal documents. See [How to detect a fake job or recruiter](../how-to-detect-a-fake-job-or-recruiter/).
