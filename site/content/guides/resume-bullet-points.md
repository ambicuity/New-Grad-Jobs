A recruiter's first pass over a new grad résumé is a few seconds long. They are not reading; they are looking for evidence that you have done the kind of work the role involves. Bullets are where that evidence lives, and most new grad bullets fail because they describe duties instead of results.

## The shape of a good bullet

Verb, what you built or did, how, and what it changed. One line, two at most.

- Weak: "Responsible for developing features for the internal dashboard."
- Better: "Built the dashboard's export feature in React and Flask, replacing a manual weekly spreadsheet for the operations team."

The second version names a thing, names the tools, and names who it was for. A reader can picture it and ask about it.

## Numbers, honestly

A number makes a bullet concrete, but only a true one. If you measured it, use it: "cut the nightly job from 40 minutes to 6". If you did not measure it, describe the change instead of inventing a percentage. Interviewers ask how numbers were obtained, and a made-up one is the fastest way to lose an offer.

Good sources of honest numbers: users or teammates who used the thing, requests or records processed, time saved on a task you timed, test coverage, size of the dataset, number of people on the team.

## Verbs that carry information

Built, designed, implemented, migrated, automated, measured, reduced, debugged, shipped, maintained, wrote, tested, presented. Avoid "helped", "assisted", "participated in" and "was responsible for"; they hide what you actually did. If you were one of four people, say what your part was.

## Coursework and projects

Projects are experience for a new graduate and get the same treatment: a name, what it does, the stack, and one thing about it that was hard or that worked. "Course project" is fine as a label, but the bullet should still describe the thing, not the course. [New grad projects](../new-grad-projects/) covers which projects are worth the space.

## Ordering

Within a role, put the bullet that best matches the target posting first. Recruiters read the first bullet of each entry and skim the rest. This is the cheapest form of tailoring and it is covered in [Tailoring your résumé](../tailoring-your-resume/).

## Things to cut

- Soft-skill claims with no evidence ("strong communicator", "fast learner")
- Duties every holder of the title had ("attended stand-ups")
- Tools listed without a use ("familiar with Docker")
- Anything from before university unless it is directly relevant

## A checklist per bullet

1. Does it start with a verb?
2. Does it name a concrete thing?
3. Would I be comfortable being asked about every word in it?
4. Could a stranger tell what changed because I did it?
5. Is it one line?
