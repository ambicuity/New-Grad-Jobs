This guide is for new graduates in their first three months of a full-time job in any field: an analyst, a nurse, an engineer, a sales representative, a lab technician. The first 90 days are mostly about learning what good work looks like on your team and making sure the people you work with can see yours. The plan below does not depend on your field or your employer's onboarding programme. What to finish before day one is in [After accepting a new grad offer](../after-accepting-a-new-grad-offer/), and the screening steps are in [Background checks and pre-employment verification](../background-checks-and-pre-employment-verification/).

## Week one: paperwork with deadlines

Some first-week paperwork has legal or employer deadlines. Do it first.

::: evidence official
USCIS says employees must complete Section 1 of Form I-9 "no later than their first day of employment," and may do so any time after accepting the offer. Employers complete Section 2 within three business days of the hire ([USCIS, completing Section 1](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/complete-correct-form-i-9/completing-section-1-employee-information-and-attestation)).
:::

- **Form I-9 (US).** You choose which documents to present from the [acceptable documents lists](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/form-i-9-acceptable-documents): one from List A, or one from List B plus one from List C. Ask HR whether documents are reviewed in person or remotely.
- **Form W-4 (US).** The IRS says to "complete Form W-4 so that your employer can withhold the correct federal income tax from your pay," and to consider a new one each year and when your situation changes ([IRS, About Form W-4](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-4)). The same page links the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator. Ask HR whether your state also has a withholding form.
- **Outside the US.** In Canada or India, ask HR which tax, payroll and identity documents they need and by when.
- **Direct deposit and emergency contact.** Submit them through the employer's verified HR portal.
- **Benefits enrollment.** Enrollment windows, coverage start dates and what happens if you miss the window are set by your employer's plans. Ask HR for the exact deadline on day one, put it in your calendar, and read the plan documents before choosing. Ask what happens if you miss it.
- **Policies you sign.** Code of conduct, IT and security, confidentiality. Keep copies, along with your signed offer letter and agreement. [Reading your employment agreement](../reading-your-employment-agreement/) explains the clauses.

If you are on OPT or STEM OPT, report your new employer to your DSO as your school directs; [OPT unemployment days](../opt-unemployment-days/) covers reporting.

## Days 1 to 30: learn the role

Your goal in the first month is to understand what you are expected to deliver and how your team works. In your first meeting with your manager, ask:

1. What does a good first 90 days look like for this role?
2. What should I be able to do on my own by the end of month one, and month three?
3. How will my work be reviewed, and when is the first formal review?
4. Who should I meet, and who do I go to for which kinds of questions?
5. How do you prefer to get updates: written, in one-on-ones, or as things happen?

Write the answers down and send a short summary back: "Thanks for today. My understanding is that by [date] I should be able to [X] and [Y]. Let me know if I've missed anything." That email becomes your reference point.

Then learn the basics of your field's workflow: the systems, the forms, the handoffs, the people who approve things. Ask questions early. Keep a running list of terms and acronyms. If there is no onboarding document, keep notes good enough to become one.

## Set up one-on-ones

A regular one-on-one with your manager is where you raise problems early and get feedback. If one is not already scheduled, ask for it: "Would a regular 30-minute check-in work for you, weekly or every two weeks?" Bring a short agenda each time:

- What I finished since last time
- What I'm working on next
- Where I'm stuck or need a decision
- One question about how I'm doing

Also set up short introductory conversations with teammates and the people your work depends on. Ask what they do, what they need from someone in your role, and what they wish new hires knew.

## Keep a record of your work (start in week one)

Start a private work log in week one and add to it every Friday. Keep it on a personal note or in a place your employer allows, and do not copy confidential information out of employer systems.

| Week | What I did | Result or evidence | Who was involved | What I learned |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [date] | [task, project or case] | [shipped, closed, approved, a number your team tracks] | [names or teams] | [skill, tool, feedback] |

The log helps in one-on-ones, in your first review, when you update your résumé, and if a question about your work ever comes up. [Résumé bullet points](../resume-bullet-points/) explains how to turn entries into evidence later.

## Days 61 to 90: ask for feedback

Do not wait for the formal review. Around day 60, ask your manager directly:

> "I'd like to check how I'm doing against what we discussed in my first week. What's one thing I should keep doing, and one thing I should change before my 90-day mark?"

Ask a teammate the same question about a specific piece of work. When you get feedback, thank them, ask one clarifying question if needed, and say what you will do about it. At day 90, compare your work log with your first-week summary and share the comparison with your manager.

## When to raise concerns

Raise a concern early when it is about the work: unclear expectations, more work than you can finish, a tool or training you need. Bring it to your manager with a specific example and a suggestion.

Some concerns need a different route. If you think you are not being paid correctly, are being asked to do something unsafe or improper, or are being treated differently because of a protected characteristic, use the channel in your employer's written policy: HR, an ethics line, or a manager other than your own. Write down dates and what happened. Outside the company, agencies may have deadlines.

::: evidence official
For US discrimination charges, the EEOC states a 180-calendar-day filing deadline from the date of the discrimination, extended to 300 calendar days where a state or local agency enforces a law against the same type of discrimination, with a narrower rule for age claims; federal employees follow a different process ([EEOC, how to file a charge](https://www.eeoc.gov/how-file-charge-employment-discrimination)).
:::

Other deadlines and agencies apply to pay, safety and to workplaces outside the US. An employment attorney or the relevant agency can tell you which applies to you.
