Requirements sections are written by many hands. A hiring manager lists what the ideal candidate has, a recruiter adds what the last hire had, and legal adds what must be true. The result reads as a wall of conditions, and new graduates, who tend to take instructions literally, rule themselves out of roles they could have had. Here is how to read the wall.

## Firm requirements

These are rarely negotiable and usually appear in their own sentence:

- **Work authorization and citizenship.** "Must be authorized to work without sponsorship" or "US citizenship required" may reflect employer policy, legal or contractual constraints. Read the exact wording and ask when it is unclear. The board flags both; see [Citizenship and clearance](../citizenship-and-clearance/).
- **Licenses and registrations.** A nursing license, a CPA track, a professional engineering exam. If the role needs one, it needs one.
- **A specific degree when the role is regulated.** Accounting, healthcare, some engineering roles.
- **Location and hours** when stated plainly. "On-site five days" means that.

## Soft requirements

Some requirements allow flexibility, but do not assume everything outside regulated eligibility is optional. Read required and preferred qualifications separately:

- **Years of experience.** "0–2 years" means none is fine. For "1–3 years", ask whether internships, research or other experience count; the title alone does not decide. "5+ years" is a mid-level role with the wrong title, and the board excludes those titles for that reason.
- **Named technologies.** A list of languages and frameworks may describe required skills, preferred skills or the team's stack. Show relevant project use and distinguish required tools from examples of the team's stack.
- **"Strong understanding of…"** is asking whether you can talk about the topic in an interview.
- **"Preferred" and "nice to have"** are exactly that.

## A practical test

Apply when you meet stated eligibility and can demonstrate the core work, even if some preferred qualifications are missing. There is no verified percentage of requirements that guarantees review.

Do not apply when a firm requirement fails. If eligibility is genuinely unclear, ask the recruiter. A prior rejection does not by itself establish a penalty on future applications.

## What the board can and cannot tell you

The board shows the title, location, company, category, a description excerpt, compensation when posted, and the two eligibility flags. It does not parse years of experience or degree requirements: postings phrase them too many ways to read reliably, and a wrong reading would hide real jobs. Read the posting.

## Two habits that help

- Keep a short written inventory of what you can evidence: languages with project names, tools from internships, coursework with outcomes. Check postings against it instead of against your confidence on a given day.
- When a requirement is unclear, apply and ask in the first conversation. Recruiters answer eligibility questions freely; they would rather not schedule an interview that cannot go anywhere.
