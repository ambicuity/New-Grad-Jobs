Behavioral interviews ask for past examples on the theory that past behavior predicts future behavior. For a new graduate the past is coursework, projects, internships, clubs and jobs of any kind, and all of that is fair material. The interview is not testing whether you have an impressive past; it is testing whether you can reflect on it honestly.

## The questions

They come in a few families, whatever the wording:

- Conflict: a disagreement with a teammate, a manager, a client
- Failure: something that went wrong and what you did
- Ownership: something you drove without being asked
- Learning: something hard you learned quickly
- Teamwork: a group result and your part in it
- Ambiguity: a task with no clear instructions
- Feedback: criticism you received and what changed

Employers that publish values (and many large ones do) ask questions mapped to them. Read the values page before the interview.

## Six stories

Prepare six stories, each usable for two or three families. For each, write down:

- **Situation**: one or two sentences of context
- **Task**: what you had to do and why it was hard
- **Action**: what you, specifically, did; this is most of the answer
- **Result**: what happened, with an honest number or outcome
- **Reflection**: what you would do differently

This is the STAR structure with a fifth step, and the fifth step is what separates a good answer from a rehearsed one. Interviewers at new grad level care more about self-awareness than about outcomes.

Stories can come from anywhere: a group project where a member disappeared, an internship bug that took a week, a club event that nearly failed, a part-time job with a difficult customer. Small, true and detailed beats large and vague.

## Answering

Answer in two minutes or less, then stop. Interviewers ask follow-ups; that is the interview working, not a sign the answer was short. Say "I", not "we", for the actions you took. If you did not take the action, pick another story.

When the honest result was bad, say so and go straight to the reflection. A failure story with a real lesson is one of the strongest answers available, and the question is usually asked precisely to see whether you can give one.

## Questions to ask

The last five minutes are yours. Ask about the team's work, how new graduates are onboarded, what the first three months look like, and how performance is reviewed. Avoid questions the careers page answers. The [Questions before accepting](../questions-before-accepting/) guide has more for the offer stage.

## Common mistakes

- Answering a "time when" question with a general description of how you usually behave
- Choosing a story that makes a teammate the villain
- Giving results you cannot back up
- Talking for five minutes
- Not preparing at all because "it is just a chat". It is scored like every other round.
